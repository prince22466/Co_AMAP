# Investigation checkpoint: 111572223 / 111574474

This note is a resume-safe checkpoint for diagnosing the two v20_3 loss histories:

- `111572223.json`
- `111574474.json`

The investigation target is to separate bad outcomes caused by:

- `production_signals`
- `animal_plan`
- `crop_plan`
- `market_orders`

and to trace event -> signal -> order/action -> realized outcome, especially:

1. when a shop opens, whether the corresponding animal/seed procurement signal appears and whether a purchase is actually attempted/executed;
2. when the opponent adds supply capacity, how our production gap and buying behavior change;
3. whether "sell immediately" is actually carried out once produce is sellable;
4. whether large gross fertilizer turnover is a true economic loss or mostly offsetting buy/sell flow;
5. whether animal losses are primarily a procurement/signaling problem, a feeding/provisioning problem, or a planner/execution problem.

## What has been examined

The existing diagnostic stack was reviewed:

- `DIAGNOSTICS.md`
- `diagnose.py`
- `summarize_diagnostics.py`
- `test_diagnose.py`

The framework is already designed for exact replay rather than heuristic log inspection:

- baseline actions and resulting states are parity-checked against the recorded history;
- market commits are instrumented at engine level;
- shop openings are isolated by removing one shop instance from the same observation and recomputing signals/orders;
- opponent producer additions are isolated similarly;
- actual successful purchases/sales are tracked rather than inferred from submitted orders;
- animal escapes, crop deaths, feed state, overflow, deployment lags, and post-worker inventories are recorded;
- immediate-selling audit compares actual post-worker sellable shed inventory with requested market sells;
- fertilizer is accounted with FIFO provenance so gross fertilizer purchasing is not mislabeled as loss;
- intervention replays exist for feed buffering, fertilizer-buy removal, opponent-capacity suppression, repeat-capacity assumptions, and forced delivery.

The regression tests also explicitly verify several semantics relevant to this investigation:

- duplicate shop openings create additional demand;
- a new opponent animal reduces same-product shortage while adding wheat/feed demand;
- a positive but fractional producer-equivalent gap is distinguished from zero demand;
- same-species replanting is treated as a new capacity event.

## Current observations to preserve

These observations were already identified before this checkpoint. Treat them as **episode observations to be engine-verified/quantified in the generated evidence report**, not as final causal conclusions:

- all four opening animals disappear before day 2;
- no wheat purchases were observed around that opening-animal failure;
- the agent repeatedly buys and sells fertilizer at very high volume;
- across the two games, 16 animal losses with about 7,600 coins of acquisition cost were observed;
- the first Farmers Market opening triggered the corresponding seed purchases one turn later;
- later shop responses were often blocked by cash or available deployment space;
- fertilizer gross purchase spend is misleading by itself because much of the cash flow is offset by fertilizer resale.

Important accounting interpretation:

- escaped-animal acquisition cost is a real sunk asset cost, but it is **not** the same as the counterfactual score gain from preventing the escape;
- repeated unsent inventory in the selling audit is measured in unit-turns and must not be summed as destroyed value;
- fertilizer must be judged by executed buy cost, resale cash, consumed purchased fertilizer, collected fertilizer, overflow, and remaining inventory together.

## Primary causal questions still to answer

### A. Shop opening -> production signal -> procurement

For every shop opening in both histories:

- identify the newly added demand product(s);
- compare `production_signals` with and without that shop instance;
- record producer-equivalent gap before/after;
- record whether the relevant animal/seed is actionable and before its buy deadline;
- record immediate `market_orders`;
- if no buy is submitted, attribute the blocking gate:
  - fixed opening behavior
  - cannot mature
  - buy deadline
  - gap below one
  - no deployment space
  - cash reserve / affordability
  - market-order slot pressure
- record first actual successful purchase after the event;
- record units bought within 24 and 72 turns;
- record deployment lag and eventual harvest/fate.

Key distinction: a correct signal can still produce no purchase because `market_orders` gates it.

### B. Opponent new supply capacity -> our buying response

For every opponent crop/animal addition:

- record capacity delta for the produced item;
- for opponent animals, also record added wheat/feed demand;
- compare producer-equivalent gap with and without the event;
- compare same-turn buy orders with and without the event;
- record whether buying is suppressed, unchanged, or increased;
- when unchanged despite a signal change, identify the dominating gate;
- separate direct signal-to-market effect from worker-plan effects.

This should establish whether the policy reacts rationally to new public supply or overreacts/underreacts.

### C. Opening animal failure

For the opening herd:

- identify species, placement time, feed/care state by day;
- determine whether any wheat existed in shed/inventories;
- determine whether `production_signals` generated wheat demand;
- determine whether `market_orders` attempted wheat procurement;
- if not, identify the exact gate or missing policy path;
- count end-of-day unfed animals;
- identify exact escape turns and acquisition cost;
- determine whether worker scheduling also failed to feed/care animals when wheat was available.

This is likely the highest-value early-game failure mode and should be separated into:
1. no feed procurement,
2. feed available but not delivered,
3. care scheduling failure,
4. some combination.

### D. Immediate-selling policy

For every normal sellable product:

- use actual post-worker shed stock;
- apply only the intended wheat/fertilizer reserves;
- compare eligible quantity with requested sells;
- compare requested sells with successful engine commits;
- quantify:
  - prediction errors,
  - eligible-but-unrequested unit-turns,
  - unfilled submitted sells,
  - inventory carried outside the shed,
  - harvest-to-sale lag,
  - cross-day delay,
  - terminal unsold harvest.

Do not infer a selling bug merely because harvested items remain in worker inventory. They are not directly sellable until delivered to the shed.

### E. Fertilizer churn

Quantify for each episode:

- purchased units and cash cost;
- collected fertilizer;
- sold units and sales cash;
- purchased units later resold;
- FIFO purchased-resale P&L;
- same-turn buy/resell count;
- purchased fertilizer consumed;
- overflow;
- ending fertilizer inventory.

Only net P&L / opportunity-cost effects should be called losses. Gross purchase volume alone is not evidence of loss.

## Existing interventions to use for causality

Run the existing interventions separately and compare both our score and margin:

- `feed_buffer`
- `fert_no_churn`
- `feed_and_fert`
- `ignore_opponent_capacity`
- `no_repeat_capacity`
- `deliver_products`

Interpretations must remain non-additive because interventions interact.

Especially useful comparisons:

- baseline vs `feed_buffer`: economic effect of adding short-horizon wheat procurement;
- baseline vs `fert_no_churn`: broad stress test for fertilizer buying/churn;
- baseline vs `deliver_products`: whether delayed transport materially hurts immediate selling;
- baseline vs `ignore_opponent_capacity`: sensitivity to public opponent supply assumptions.
